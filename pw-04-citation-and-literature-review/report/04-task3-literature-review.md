# Task 3. Literature review: related works

## Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) based on the Transformer architecture [@vaswani2017] have become general-purpose tools for text understanding and generation, and they can solve new tasks from a few examples in the prompt [@brown2020]. For organisations, however, a model alone is not enough: it does not know the organisation's current documents and cannot act in its information systems. Two research directions address these limitations — retrieval-augmented generation, which supplies the model with relevant documents [@lewis2020], and tool use, which lets the model call external functions and continue reasoning with their results [@yao2023react; @schick2023]. Combined in a loop of reasoning, acting and observing, they form *LLM-based agents* [@wang2024survey; @xi2025]. Since November 2024, the Model Context Protocol (MCP) has offered an open standard for connecting such agents to tools and data [@anthropic2024mcp; @mcp_spec].

The objective of this research is to design and evaluate an agentic AI platform that integrates the agent loop, MCP-based tools and RAG, and to compare architectural options by task success, answer faithfulness, integration effort, cost and security. This section reviews related works on retrieval, tool use and agents, evaluation, security and the Kazakhstani context.

## Related works on retrieval-augmented generation

Lewis et al. [@lewis2020] proposed RAG, in which a sequence-to-sequence generator is combined with a dense retriever over a Wikipedia index; the model produced more specific and factual answers than a purely parametric model, and its knowledge could be updated by replacing the index. Dense retrieval itself was popularised by Karpukhin et al. [@karpukhin2020], whose dual-encoder model outperformed the classical BM25 ranking function [@robertson2009] on open-domain question answering. Efficient approximate nearest-neighbour search, such as hierarchical navigable small world graphs [@malkov2020] and GPU-based similarity search [@johnson2021], made dense retrieval practical for large collections.

Surveys show that RAG has evolved from a simple "retrieve-then-read" pipeline to modular systems with query rewriting, hybrid retrieval, reranking and iterative retrieval [@gao2023; @fan2024]. Asai et al. [@asai2024] trained a model to decide when retrieval is needed and to critique its own generations, improving factuality and citation accuracy. Graph-based RAG builds a knowledge graph from documents to answer global, summary-type questions [@edge2024]. At the same time, Liu et al. [@liu2024lost] showed that models use information at the beginning and end of long contexts much better than information in the middle, which means that more retrieved text is not always better. For Kazakh, open-domain question-answering resources have only recently appeared [@yeshpanov2024kazqad], and benchmarks show that LLMs still have gaps in Kazakh-language and regional knowledge [@togmanov2025].

## Related works on tool use and the agent loop

Wei et al. [@wei2022] showed that prompting a model to write intermediate reasoning steps improves performance on complex tasks. Yao et al. [@yao2023react] extended this idea in ReAct, where reasoning traces are interleaved with actions such as search queries, and observations from the environment are fed back to the model; this loop reduced hallucinations and improved results on interactive tasks. Schick et al. [@schick2023] demonstrated that a language model can learn in a self-supervised way when to call tools such as a calculator, a search engine or a calendar.

Subsequent works scaled tool use. Gorilla [@patil2024] connected a model to a large collection of APIs and used retrieval of API documentation to reduce hallucinated calls, while ToolLLM [@qin2024] built an instruction dataset over more than 16 000 real REST APIs and showed that open models can approach proprietary models in tool use. Shinn et al. [@shinn2023] added verbal self-reflection, letting agents learn from failed attempts without updating model weights. Multi-agent frameworks such as AutoGen [@wu2024] compose several conversing agents with tools. Surveys summarise these approaches as profile, memory, planning and action modules of an agent [@wang2024survey] and describe the broader potential and risks of LLM-based agents [@xi2025; @mialon2023].

The Model Context Protocol standardises the interface between agents and tools: an MCP server exposes tools, resources and prompts, and any MCP-compatible client can discover and call them [@mcp_spec]. Hou et al. [@hou2026] analysed the MCP architecture, the life cycle of MCP servers and the ecosystem that has formed around them. Recent benchmarks evaluate agents directly on real MCP servers and complex multi-step tasks [@luo2025; @wang2025mcpbench].

## Related works on evaluation and reliability

Evaluation remains a central challenge. AgentBench [@liu2024agentbench] evaluated many LLMs in interactive environments and found a large gap between commercial and open models, with failures caused by poor long-term reasoning and instruction following. SWE-bench [@jimenez2024] showed how difficult real software-engineering tasks are for agents, and τ-bench [@yao2025tau] demonstrated that even the best tool-calling agents solve fewer than half of realistic customer-service tasks and behave inconsistently over repeated trials. For RAG, Es et al. [@es2024] proposed RAGAS, a set of reference-free metrics for faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance. Zheng et al. [@zheng2023] showed that strong LLMs can act as judges with good agreement with human preferences, but also documented their biases, which means that automatic judgements must be validated.

## Related works on security

Greshake et al. [@greshake2023] introduced *indirect prompt injection*: an attacker places instructions in data that an LLM-integrated application later retrieves, such as a web page or an e-mail, and the model follows them. They showed data theft, manipulation of answers and misuse of tools in real applications. Tool ecosystems amplify this risk: Radosevich and Halloran [@radosevich2025] demonstrated that LLMs connected through MCP can be coerced into malicious actions such as accessing credentials, and Hou et al. [@hou2026] systematised MCP threats such as spoofed servers and poisoned tool descriptions. Industry guidance lists prompt injection, excessive agency and sensitive-information disclosure among the top risks of LLM applications [@owasp2025llm].

## Summary of the review

The reviewed works show that (1) RAG and tool use substantially extend what LLMs can do; (2) the agent loop of reasoning, acting and observing is the common basis of modern agents; (3) the reliability of agents on realistic multi-step tasks is still low, and evaluation methods are fragmented; (4) tool-using agents are exposed to indirect prompt injection, and MCP creates new attack surfaces; and (5) evidence for Kazakh- and Russian-language institutional data is scarce. The main open problem for this research is the lack of an integrated, evaluated platform architecture in which the agent loop, MCP tools and RAG are combined with guardrails and measured together. This defines the objective of the planned study: to build such a platform and to compare retrieval configurations, agent-loop designs, integration through MCP versus bespoke code, and guardrails against prompt injection, using a bilingual university document corpus and repeated, cost-aware experiments.
